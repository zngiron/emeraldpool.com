Cold weather is not a reason to drain the spa and put the cover away for the season. It is the season the thing was bought for. A hot tub that stays filled and heated through an Oregon winter costs less to run than one you shut down and restart, and it is the only time of year when a twenty-minute soak genuinely changes how the evening goes.

Wear a hat

It sounds like a joke and it is the single most useful piece of advice on this page. Most of the heat you lose sitting in a spa on a thirty-degree night goes out of the top of your head. A knit hat keeps your hair dry and your body temperature steady, and it is the difference between fifteen minutes and forty.

Go easy on the blower

Anything that injects air into the water drops the water temperature quickly, and the air blower is the worst offender in winter. Use it in bursts rather than continuously, and make sure it is off when you get out. The jets are fine; it is the air that costs you.

Moisturise before, not after

Dry winter air plus hot water is what makes skin feel tight afterwards, and that is what stops people using the spa as often as they meant to. A thin layer of face moisturiser before you go out keeps the worst of it off and stops spa chemistry sitting in your pores.

Stock the chemicals before the road ices

The week you run out of sanitiser is the week the pass closes. Check the cupboard in November: sanitiser, shock, a spare filter, and test strips that are not two years old. Both stores test water for free if you would rather bring a sample than guess.

Check the water level weekly

A low level in freezing weather is how pumps get damaged — the one winter failure that turns a cheap season into an expensive one. Thirty seconds under the cover once a week is the whole job.

If something is not behaving, ring the nearer store rather than waiting for spring. Most winter calls we take are a filter or a cover, and both are same-week fixes.
